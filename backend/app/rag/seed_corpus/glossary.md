# finzorr finance glossary — original explanations, maintainer-authored.
# Format: "## Term | category" followed by the explanation body.

## P/E Ratio (Price-to-Earnings) | glossary
The P/E ratio divides a company's share price by its earnings per share. It tells you how many rupees investors are paying today for one rupee of annual profit. A high P/E often signals high growth expectations; a low P/E can mean the stock is cheap — or that the market expects trouble. Compare P/E within the same sector, not across unrelated industries.

## EPS (Earnings Per Share) | glossary
EPS is a company's net profit divided by its number of outstanding shares. It is the basic building block of most valuation metrics: growing EPS over years usually indicates a healthy, expanding business. Watch for EPS growth driven by buybacks rather than actual profit growth.

## Market Capitalization | glossary
Market cap is the share price multiplied by the total number of shares — the market's price tag for the whole company. In India, large-caps are roughly the top 100 companies by market cap, mid-caps the next 150, and the rest are small-caps. Size affects volatility: small-caps tend to swing harder in both directions.

## P/B Ratio (Price-to-Book) | glossary
The P/B ratio compares the share price to the company's book value (assets minus liabilities) per share. It matters most for banks and asset-heavy businesses where book value approximates real economic value. A P/B below 1 means the market prices the company below its net assets — sometimes a bargain, sometimes a warning.

## Dividend Yield | glossary
Dividend yield is the annual dividend per share divided by the share price, expressed as a percentage. A 3% yield means ₹3 paid out yearly for every ₹100 invested at today's price. High yields can reward patient investors, but an unusually high yield sometimes signals a falling stock price rather than generous payouts.

## ROE (Return on Equity) | glossary
ROE measures how much profit a company generates per rupee of shareholders' equity. Consistently high ROE (say above 15%) suggests an efficient, competitively strong business. Always check whether high ROE comes from genuine profitability or from heavy debt inflating the ratio.

## Book Value | glossary
Book value is what would notionally remain for shareholders if a company sold all assets and paid all debts — its accounting net worth. Book value per share anchors the P/B ratio. It understates businesses rich in brands or software, whose real value lives outside the balance sheet.

## Beta | glossary
Beta measures how strongly a stock moves relative to the overall market. A beta of 1 moves with the index; above 1 amplifies market swings; below 1 dampens them. Defensive sectors like FMCG usually carry low betas, while cyclical and small-cap names often exceed 1.

## 52-Week High/Low | glossary
The 52-week high and low mark the highest and lowest prices a stock touched over the past year. Traders use them as psychological reference points: breakouts above the 52-week high often attract momentum buyers, while stocks near their lows invite bargain-hunting — and value traps.

## Face Value | glossary
Face value is the nominal value printed on a share (commonly ₹1, ₹2, ₹5, or ₹10 in India). Dividends are often declared as a percentage of face value — "100% dividend" on a ₹2 face value means ₹2 per share, not double your money. It has little to do with the market price.

## Nifty 50 | glossary
The Nifty 50 is the National Stock Exchange's benchmark index: 50 of India's largest, most liquid companies weighted by free-float market cap. It is the default yardstick for "the Indian market" — index funds track it, and fund managers are judged against it.

## Sensex | glossary
The Sensex (S&P BSE Sensex) is the Bombay Stock Exchange's 30-company benchmark index, India's oldest equity index. Sensex and Nifty usually move together; the difference is the exchange and the number of constituents, not the underlying economics.

## NSE and BSE | glossary
The National Stock Exchange (NSE) and Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE) are India's two main stock exchanges. Most large companies list on both, and prices stay nearly identical thanks to arbitrage. NSE dominates derivatives volume; BSE hosts thousands of additional small listings.

## Circuit Limits | glossary
Circuit limits cap how far a stock or the whole market can move in one session (commonly 5%, 10%, or 20% for stocks). When the band is hit, trading in that security pauses or freezes at the band. They exist to interrupt panic spirals and give participants time to absorb news.

## T+1 Settlement | glossary
T+1 settlement means shares and money change hands one business day after the trade. India moved to T+1 ahead of most major markets. Practical effect: sell shares today, and the cash is available tomorrow.

## Demat Account | investing_basics
A demat (dematerialized) account holds your shares electronically, the way a bank account holds money. In India you need a demat account with a depository participant (broker) to buy or sell listed shares; physical share certificates are history.

## SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) | investing_basics
A SIP invests a fixed amount into a mutual fund at a regular interval, typically monthly. It automates discipline and averages your purchase price across market ups and downs (rupee-cost averaging), removing the temptation to time the market.

## Mutual Fund | investing_basics
A mutual fund pools money from many investors and hands it to a professional manager who buys a diversified basket of securities. You own units of the pool. Costs matter: the expense ratio silently compounds against you, which is why low-cost index funds are popular defaults.

## Index Fund | investing_basics
An index fund simply copies an index like the Nifty 50 instead of paying a manager to pick stocks. Because most active funds fail to beat the index after fees over long periods, low-cost index funds are a strong default for long-term investors.

## ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund) | investing_basics
An ETF is a fund that trades on the exchange like a single stock. It combines index-fund diversification with intraday tradability. Check liquidity: thinly traded ETFs can drift from their underlying value.

## Diversification | investing_basics
Diversification spreads money across companies, sectors, and asset classes so no single failure sinks the portfolio. It is the one "free lunch" in investing: it lowers risk without necessarily lowering expected returns. Over-diversification, though, dilutes your best ideas into index-like returns with extra cost.

## IPO (Initial Public Offering) | investing_basics
An IPO is a private company's first sale of shares to the public, after which the stock lists on the exchange. In India, retail investors apply through ASBA/UPI and shares are allotted by lottery when oversubscribed. Listing-day pops get the headlines; long-term IPO performance is far more mixed.

## Blue-Chip Stocks | investing_basics
Blue-chips are large, established, financially sound companies with long records of stable earnings — the Reliances, TCSes, and HDFC Banks. They anchor conservative portfolios: usually slower growth than small-caps, but far fewer existential surprises.

## Stop-Loss | investing_basics
A stop-loss is a standing order to sell if the price falls to a level you set — a pre-commitment that caps your downside. Traders lean on it heavily; long-term investors use it sparingly. In fast crashes gaps can execute your stop well below the trigger.

## Intraday vs Delivery Trading | investing_basics
Intraday trades open and close within the same session — you never own the shares overnight. Delivery means holding shares in your demat beyond the session. Intraday allows leverage and short-selling but demands constant attention; delivery suits investors with horizons longer than a day.

## Bull and Bear Markets | glossary
A bull market is a sustained rise in prices with optimistic sentiment; a bear market is commonly defined as a fall of 20% or more from a recent high. The labels matter because strategy differs: bull markets forgive mistakes, bear markets punish leverage and reward patience.

## Volatility | glossary
Volatility measures how violently a price swings, regardless of direction. India VIX gauges expected near-term volatility of the Nifty. High volatility means wider outcomes both ways — it is risk to a short-term trader and often opportunity to a long-term buyer.

## Liquidity | glossary
Liquidity is how easily you can buy or sell without moving the price. Large-caps absorb crores of order flow with tiny impact; micro-caps may move several percent on a modest order. Illiquidity compounds losses precisely when you most need an exit.

## RSI (Relative Strength Index) | technical_indicator
RSI is a momentum oscillator from 0 to 100 that compares recent gains against recent losses (typically over 14 periods). Readings above 70 conventionally flag overbought conditions and below 30 oversold. In strong trends, RSI can stay "overbought" for months — treat it as context, not a trigger.

## MACD | technical_indicator
MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence) tracks the gap between a fast and a slow exponential moving average, plus a signal line of that gap. Crossovers and divergences hint at momentum shifts. Like all trend-following tools it whipsaws in sideways markets.

## Moving Averages | technical_indicator
A moving average smooths price over a window — 50-day and 200-day are the classics. Price above a rising 200-day average is a common definition of a long-term uptrend; the "golden cross" (50-day crossing above the 200-day) and "death cross" (the reverse) are widely watched signals.

## Support and Resistance | technical_indicator
Support is a price zone where falling stocks repeatedly find buyers; resistance is where rallies repeatedly stall. These levels emerge from crowd memory of prior transactions. Breaks of well-watched levels often accelerate moves as stops trigger and sidelined traders chase.

## Volume | technical_indicator
Volume is the number of shares traded in a period. Price moves on heavy volume carry more conviction than moves on thin volume. A breakout without volume is a rumor; with volume, it is a statement.

## Candlestick Charts | technical_indicator
Each candlestick shows open, high, low, and close for a period; the body shows open-to-close, the wicks show extremes. Patterns like doji or engulfing candles compress crowd psychology into single bars. They describe probabilities, never certainties.

## Futures and Options (F&O) | glossary
Futures oblige you to buy or sell at a set price on a set date; options give the right without the obligation, for an upfront premium. In India F&O is cash-settled and hugely popular — and leverage means small price moves produce outsized gains and losses. SEBI data shows most retail F&O traders lose money.

## Call and Put Options | glossary
A call option profits when the underlying rises above the strike plus premium; a put profits when it falls below the strike minus premium. Buyers risk only the premium; sellers collect the premium but carry potentially large obligations. Time decay silently erodes every option's value daily.

## Lot Size | glossary
Derivatives trade in fixed bundles called lots — you cannot buy one Nifty future, only whole lots. Exchanges set lot sizes so each contract stays near a target notional value, revising them as prices move.

## Margin | glossary
Margin is the collateral your broker blocks when you take leveraged positions. If the market moves against you, marks-to-market losses eat the margin, triggering calls for more funds — and forced liquidation if you cannot pay. Leverage cuts both ways, faster than intuition suggests.

## Short Selling | glossary
Short selling means selling shares you do not own, hoping to buy them back cheaper. In Indian cash markets, retail short positions must square off intraday; multi-day shorts use futures or options. Losses on shorts are theoretically unlimited because prices have no ceiling.

## FII and DII | glossary
FIIs (foreign institutional investors) and DIIs (domestic institutions like mutual funds, insurers, and pension funds) move markets with their daily flows, published each evening. Sustained FII selling absorbed by DII buying has been a defining pattern of recent Indian market cycles.

## SEBI | regulatory
SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) regulates Indian securities markets: exchanges, brokers, mutual funds, and listed companies. It writes disclosure rules, polices insider trading and manipulation, and runs investor-protection programs. Registered intermediaries and grievance channels (SCORES) exist because of SEBI mandates.

## KYC (Know Your Customer) | regulatory
KYC is the mandatory identity-verification process — PAN, address proof, and in-person or video verification — before you can open a demat or trading account. It exists to prevent fraud and money laundering. Without completed KYC, no Indian broker can activate your account.

## Insider Trading | regulatory
Insider trading is dealing in a company's shares while holding unpublished price-sensitive information. SEBI prohibits it, monitors trading patterns around announcements, and levies heavy penalties. Company insiders must disclose trades and observe trading-window blackouts.

## Pump and Dump | regulatory
A pump-and-dump artificially inflates a thinly traded stock through hype — social media tips, Telegram groups, circular trading — then dumps holdings on latecomers. Low-liquidity small-caps are the usual vehicles. Unsolicited "sure-shot" tips are the classic red flag.

## Compounding | investing_basics
Compounding is earning returns on past returns. Its power grows non-linearly with time: money doubling every 7 years turns ₹1 lakh into ₹8 lakh in 21 years, with most of the gain arriving late. Starting early beats starting big — time in the market outweighs timing the market.

## Inflation and Real Returns | investing_basics
Real return is your return after subtracting inflation. A 7% fixed deposit against 6% inflation grows purchasing power by roughly 1%, not 7%. Equities' long-run appeal is precisely their historical ability to outrun inflation, at the cost of short-term volatility.

## Asset Allocation | investing_basics
Asset allocation is the split of your money across equity, debt, gold, and cash. It typically explains far more of a portfolio's outcome than individual stock picks. The right mix depends on horizon and temperament; rebalancing back to targets forces the discipline of selling high and buying low.
